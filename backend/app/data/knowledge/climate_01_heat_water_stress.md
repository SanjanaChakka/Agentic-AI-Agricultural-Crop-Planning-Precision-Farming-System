# Heat stress and water stress

Heat stress occurs when air or canopy temperature exceeds the crop's
heat-tolerant ceiling for long enough to disrupt growth. Broad working
thresholds used by this system:

- Above 35 degrees C: heat stress possible for most crops.
- Above 40 degrees C: severe heat stress; pollen viability and photosynthesis
  fall sharply in cereals.
- Wheat is affected earlier - flowering and grain filling are damaged above
  about 30 degrees C.
- Millets, sorghum and cotton tolerate more heat than wheat, maize, rice and
  potato.

Consequences of heat stress: reduced photosynthesis, increased respiration,
flower and fruit drop, poor pollen viability, poor grain filling, higher crop
water demand. Heat and drought interact: hot dry weather closes stomata, so
the crop transpires less but suffers more, and a single irrigation may not
relieve stress for as long as expected.

Water stress occurs when water uptake cannot meet evapotranspiration. Symptoms
are wilting, leaf rolling, leaf area reduction and, in cereals, a silvery
appearance. Water stress reduces nutrient uptake, pollination and root growth,
and is the single largest yield limiter in rainfed systems.

Combined heat and water stress is the most damaging combination in irrigated
crops, because the crop needs more water precisely when supply is hardest and
when pumping capacity may be reduced.

Monitoring guidance:

- Use forecast evapotranspiration (ET0 x Kc) together with measured soil
  moisture; a single hot day with normal soil moisture is not water stress.
- A dry soil profile plus forecast heat and no rainfall is a genuine water
  stress warning even when the crop looks healthy, because canopy wilting lags
  behind root-zone depletion.
- Recovery after irrigation is slower under heat stress; do not interpret slow
  recovery as a sensor fault without checking other diagnostics.
